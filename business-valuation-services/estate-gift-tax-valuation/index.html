Definition & Authority

What Is an Estate or Gift Tax Valuation?

An estate or gift tax valuation is an independent appraisal of the fair market value of a closely held business interest for a gift, estate, or trust transfer, prepared under IRS rules. The governing standard is fair market value as defined in Revenue Ruling 59-60: the price at which the interest would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, both reasonably informed and neither under compulsion.

When you gift or inherit an interest in a private company, the IRS needs a supportable number, not an estimate. A documented valuation sets the value reported on a gift or estate tax return, supports the discounts you claim, and gives you a conclusion that holds up if the return is examined. It is the difference between a figure you can defend and one you have to walk back.

IRS Standard of Value

How We Determine Fair Market Value

We value the interest to the fair market value standard set in Revenue Ruling 59-60, which directs an appraiser to weigh eight foundational factors — among them the nature and history of the business, economic outlook, book value, earning capacity, dividend-paying capacity, goodwill and intangibles, prior sales of stock, and market prices of comparable publicly traded companies.

From there we apply the three recognized approaches to value: the income approach, the market approach, and the asset approach, reconciling them into one supported conclusion rather than an ambiguous range.

Discounts & Chapter 14

Valuation Discounts for Estate & Gift Tax

Closely held business interests rarely transfer at their pro-rata share of total enterprise value. Two discounts drive most adjustments, and both must be rigorously supported with empirical market data:

Discount for Lack of Marketability (DLOM)

Reflects the inability of an owner in a private company to convert their holding into cash quickly and without substantial transaction costs. We analyze restricted stock studies, pre-IPO studies, and option-pricing volatility models rather than applying flat rules of thumb.

Explore Our DLOM Explainer →

Minority / Lack-of-Control Discount (DLOC)

Accounts for a non-controlling owner's inability to direct distributions, declare dividends, hire or fire management, force a sale, or liquidate the business. Derived from observed control premiums in public and private acquisitions.

Read Minority Discount Guide →

Blockage & Special Disregarded Restrictions (IRC § 2704)

A blockage discount can apply to large, concentrated blocks that cannot be liquidated without depressing prevailing market prices. Intra-family transfers must also adhere strictly to IRC Chapter 14 (§§ 2701–2704) guardrails, specifically Section 2704 restrictions on artificial transfer limits. We quantify every adjustment with peer-reviewed empirical benchmarks, because unsupported discounts are the first thing an examiner challenges.

Regulatory Protection

IRS Compliance & Qualified Appraisal Standards

For estate and gift transfers, the appraisal itself has to meet explicit Treasury Regulation definitions. A qualified appraisal must be prepared by a qualified appraiser — professionals holding accredited credentials such as ABV (Accredited in Business Valuation) and ASA (Accredited Senior Appraiser) — following recognized national standards such as USPAP.

Adequate Disclosure Safe Harbor

A qualified appraisal attached to Form 709 satisfies Treas. Reg. § 301.6501(c)-1 adequate disclosure requirements, starting the 3-year statute of limitations so the IRS cannot audit or revalue the gift indefinitely.

IRC § 6695A Penalty Protection

Proper appraisal documentation protects both the taxpayer and counsel from substantial valuation misstatement penalties (IRC § 6662) and preparer penalties under IRC § 6695A.

Section 2032 Alternate Valuation

For estates where asset values declined following death, we evaluate the alternate valuation date 6 months post-death, modeling both scenarios so executors make tax-optimal decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions: Estate & Gift Tax

What is fair market value for estate tax purposes? +

Fair market value is the IRS standard defined in Revenue Ruling 59-60: the price at which an interest would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, both reasonably informed and neither under any compulsion to act. It is the only standard the IRS accepts for estate and gift tax reporting. For a closely held business, reaching it takes a documented analysis of the company's earnings, assets, and comparable transactions, not a rule-of-thumb multiple.

How are business interests valued for a gift? +

The appraiser values the specific interest being gifted as of the gift date, to the fair market value standard. That means applying the income, market, and asset approaches, then adjusting for the interest's characteristics — chiefly lack of marketability and, for non-controlling stakes, lack of control. The conclusion is reported on Form 709 and should be backed by a qualified appraisal so the gift qualifies for adequate disclosure.

What valuation discounts apply to estate and gift transfers? +

The two most common are the discount for lack of marketability (DLOM) and the minority or lack-of-control discount (DLOC). A blockage discount can apply to very large holdings. Each discount must be supported with empirical data and documented reasoning, and intra-family transfers must respect the Chapter 14 rules (IRC 2701–2704), including the Section 2704 limits on restrictions that would otherwise inflate the discount.

What makes an appraisal “qualified” for the IRS? +

A qualified appraisal is prepared by a qualified appraiser — one with recognized credentials such as ABV or ASA and relevant experience — follows accepted standards like USPAP, and includes the specific content the regulations require. A qualified appraisal attached to a gift return supports adequate disclosure, which starts the statute of limitations, and helps shield against the Section 6695A appraiser penalty.

What is the difference between a Form 706 and a Form 709 valuation? +

Form 709 reports a gift made during life, valuing the interest as of the gift date. Form 706 is the estate tax return, valuing the interest as of the date of death (or the alternate valuation date). The valuation standard is the same for both, but the timing, the applicable discounts, and the surrounding documentation differ, so the two returns call for different engagement planning.

What is the alternate valuation date? +

Under Section 2032, an estate may elect to value assets six months after the date of death instead of on the date of death. The election applies to the whole estate, not a single asset, and can only be made if it reduces both the gross estate and the estate tax. It is most useful when values have fallen during that window. We model both dates so the executor can make an informed election.
